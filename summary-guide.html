Weekly summary guide

How to complete Worksheet 5A for each article you read

Before you start 1 Reading notes 2 Summary paragraph Example 3 Reference 4 Checklist Download Worksheet 5A (Word)

Before you start

Complete one Worksheet 5A for each article you read. You do not need to understand every part of the article. You only need to fill in the boxes on the sheet, from top to bottom.

論文1本につきワークシート5Aを1枚書きます。論文をすべて理解する必要はありません。上から順番に欄を埋めていきましょう。

The sheet has four parts. Click a part to jump to its instructions.

Name · Date · Article no.
1Reading notes8 short notes, English or Japanese 2Summary paragraph6 sentences in English, 100–130 words 3Reference (APA 7)The full reference for the article 4Checklist6 boxes to tick before you submit

Fill in Article no. with the number of this article in your reading list (1 for your first article, 2 for your second, and so on). Use the same number in your research log. Your summaries become the first draft of your literature review, so keep every sheet.

1Reading notes

Start with the Abstract. It usually answers the first five rows. Then check the other sections to confirm. Write short phrases, not full sentences. English or Japanese is fine.

Row on the sheetWhat to writeWhere to look
Who and when?Authors' family names and the year (誰が・いつ)Title page
What did they study?The topic or research question (何を調べたか)Abstract; end of Introduction (“The purpose of this study is…”, “This study investigates…”)
How? (method)Questionnaire, interview, test, experiment, observation, or texts (方法)Abstract; Method
ParticipantsWho they were, how many, and where (参加者・人数・場所)Method → Participants
Main finding (1–2)The one or two most important results (主な結果)Abstract; Results; first paragraph of Discussion
One limitationOne specific weakness or surprising point (限界・意外な点)Discussion → “Limitations”; your own thinking
What does it mean?What the authors think the results show (結果の意味)Discussion; Conclusion
Why does it matter for my study?The connection to your own topic (自分の研究との関係)You decide
If you cannot find an answer, write “not clear” and ask in class. That is a useful question, not a failure.

2Summary paragraph

Turn your notes into six English sentences, one per box. Each box shows a grey sentence starter. You can use it, or choose another phrase from the list below the box.

Use the author's family name only (Tanaka, not Yuki Tanaka). For two authors, write “Tanaka and Smith (2021)”. For three or more, write “Tanaka et al. (2021)”.

Box 1 — Who and what (誰が・何を)

On your sheet
[Author] ([Year]) investigated / examined …
  • investigated / examined / looked at [topic]
  • explored how / whether …
  • compared [A] and [B]

Box 2 — How (方法・参加者)

On your sheet
They gave … to [number] [participants] …
MethodPhrase
Questionnaire / surveygave a questionnaire to 120 university students
Interviewinterviewed 8 high school English teachers
Testgave a vocabulary test to 60 junior high school students
Experimentcompared two groups of students: one group …, and the other group …
Observationobserved 12 English classes at …
Texts / corpusanalyzed 200 essays written by …

Add the context if you can: in Japan, at one university, over one semester.

Box 3 — Main finding (主な結果)

On your sheet
They found that …
  • The results showed that …
  • Students who … were more/less likely to …
  • There was no clear difference between … and …
  • Optional second sentence: They also found that …

Choose the one or two most important findings. Do not list every number.

Box 4 — However (限界・意外な点)

On your sheet
However, … . Therefore, …
TypeExample
Small sampleHowever, the study included only 30 students, so the results may not apply to other learners.
One contextHowever, all participants were from one university, so the results may be different in other schools.
Self-reportHowever, the data came from a questionnaire, so it shows what students think, not what they actually do.
Short timeHowever, the study lasted only four weeks, so it is unclear whether the effect continues.
Not studiedHowever, the study did not look at …, which is important because …
Unexpected resultHowever, the results for … were not clear / were different from what the authors expected.

Name the specific problem. “However, the study has some limitations” is not enough.

Box 5 — Suggests (結果の意味)

On your sheet
This suggests that …
  • These results suggest that …
  • This may mean that …
  • This study indicates that …, but more research is needed on …

Use suggests or may. Do not use “proves”. One study cannot prove something.

Box 6 — My study (自分の研究との関係)

On your sheet
This is relevant to my study because …
  • This is relevant to my study because I also want to look at …
  • This study is useful for my research because it uses a … that I could use.
  • Unlike this study, my study will focus on …
Grammar tip: Use the past tense for what the researchers did and found (examined, gave, found). Use the present tense for what the study means now (This suggests, This is relevant).

Example: a completed summary

This example uses an invented study to show what the six boxes look like when they are filled in. Do not cite it.

BoxSentence
1 Who and whatTanaka (2021) examined whether reading graded readers outside class helped Japanese university students feel more confident about reading in English.
2 HowShe gave a questionnaire to 85 first-year students at one university in Osaka.
3 Main findingShe found that students who read more than five books in one semester reported higher reading confidence than students who read fewer books.
4 HoweverHowever, the data came only from a questionnaire, so the study shows how confident students felt, not whether their reading ability actually improved.
5 SuggestsThis suggests that extensive reading may help students feel more comfortable with English texts, but more research using reading tests is needed.
6 My studyThis is relevant to my study because I want to find out whether extensive reading affects reading speed, which can be measured directly.

Read together, the six boxes make one paragraph (about 125 words):

Tanaka (2021) examined whether reading graded readers outside class helped Japanese university students feel more confident about reading in English. She gave a questionnaire to 85 first-year students at one university in Osaka. She found that students who read more than five books in one semester reported higher reading confidence than students who read fewer books. However, the data came only from a questionnaire, so the study shows how confident students felt, not whether their reading ability actually improved. This suggests that extensive reading may help students feel more comfortable with English texts, but more research using reading tests is needed. This is relevant to my study because I want to find out whether extensive reading affects reading speed, which can be measured directly.

Notice how Box 4 leads to Box 6: the limitation in Tanaka's study is the reason the student's own study is useful.

3Reference (APA 7)

Write the full reference for the article in the Reference box. Use this pattern:

Author, A. A., & Author, B. B. (Year). Title of article. Journal Name, Volume(Issue), pages. https://doi.org/…

Example:

Tanaka, Y. (2021). Graded readers and reading confidence in a Japanese university. Journal of Extensive Reading Studies, 12(3), 45–60. https://doi.org/xxxx

  • Family name first, then initials: Tanaka, Y.
  • Article title in sentence case: only the first word and names have capital letters.
  • Journal name and volume number in italics.
  • Include the DOI if the article has one. You can usually find it on the first page.
Most journal websites and Google Scholar have a “Cite” button that shows the APA reference. Copy it, then check it against the pattern above.

4Checklist

Before you submit, tick each box on your sheet. You can also tick them here while you check.
